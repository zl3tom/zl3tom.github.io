What Does 73 Mean? Amateur Radio Slang Explained

What 73, 88, QTH, QSO, QSL, QRZ, QRM, QRN, RST, CQ and other ham radio terms mean, with simple examples for new amateur radio operators.

← All guides

What does 73 mean in amateur radio?

73 means “best regards” and is one of the most common expressions in amateur radio. Operators often use it when finishing a contact, in messages and when signing an amateur-radio email. You normally say “seven three”, not “seventy-three”.

EXAMPLE
Thanks for the QSO. 73 from ZL3TOM.

What does 88 mean?

88 traditionally means “love and kisses”. It is much less universal than 73, so use it only when it suits the relationship and context.

Common Q codes and radio terms

TermPlain-English meaning
CQA general call inviting any station to reply.
QSOA radio contact or conversation between stations.
QTHYour location or station location.
QSLConfirmation that information or a contact has been received; also used for QSL cards and electronic confirmations.
QRZ?“Who is calling me?” It is also the name of the well-known callsign database QRZ.com.
QRMInterference from other stations or human-made radio signals.
QRNNatural atmospheric/static noise affecting reception.
QSYChange frequency.
QRPLow-power operation.
QRTStop transmitting / close the station.

RST signal reports

RST stands for Readability, Strength and Tone. Voice contacts commonly use the first two values, such as “five nine”. CW contacts can use all three. A 59 report means readability 5 and signal strength 9; it should describe the signal you actually receive rather than being an automatic greeting.

Other words beginners hear

DX generally means long-distance radio communication. A net is an organised on-air gathering. An OM is an old traditional abbreviation for “old man” used between operators, while YL means “young lady”. Elmer is an informal term for an experienced amateur who mentors another operator. 73s is often heard conversationally, although the established radiotelegraph abbreviation is simply 73.

Do you need to use radio slang?

No. Clear everyday language is usually better than forcing Q codes into a voice conversation. Learn the common terms so you understand other operators, but do not worry about sounding like an expert on your first contact.
