Amateur Radio Callsigns Explained: Prefixes, Suffixes and New Zealand Callsigns

Learn how amateur radio callsigns work, including prefixes, numbers, suffixes, New Zealand ZL callsigns, portable and mobile identifiers, and how to read international callsigns.

← All guides

What is an amateur radio callsign?

A callsign is the on-air identifier assigned to an amateur-radio station or operator under the relevant licensing system. Callsigns let other operators identify who they are communicating with and often provide a clue about the country or territory associated with the station.

Breaking a callsign into parts

A callsign usually contains a prefix, a numeral and a suffix. In ZL3TOM, “ZL” identifies the New Zealand callsign series, “3” is the numeral, and “TOM” is the suffix. The exact meaning and allocation rules vary between administrations, so do not assume the numeral always identifies a present-day physical district.

New Zealand callsigns

New Zealand amateur callsigns commonly use the ZL prefix, with other prefixes or special-event callsigns also possible under New Zealand arrangements. For current licensing and callsign rules, use New Zealand's official radio-spectrum information and recognised amateur-radio organisations rather than relying on an old callsign list.

International callsigns

Prefixes are allocated internationally in blocks. Familiar examples include ZL for New Zealand stations, VK for Australia and many W, K, N and AA–AL series calls in the United States. A prefix is useful for recognising a likely country or territory, but special-event, reciprocal and portable operation can make the real operating location different.

Portable and mobile identifiers

You may hear operators describe themselves as portable, mobile or operating from another location. Written forms such as /P and /M are commonly seen, but the identification requirements that legally apply depend on the licensing jurisdiction and circumstances. Follow the rules that apply to your station rather than assuming one worldwide format.

How to say a callsign clearly

Use the ITU phonetic alphabet when conditions are difficult. ZL3TOM can be spoken as Zulu Lima Three Tango Oscar Mike. Say it at a natural pace and repeat it when needed.

Why did I hear a different prefix?

An operator may be visiting another country, using a special-event callsign, participating in a contest or operating under reciprocal arrangements. Listen carefully to the complete identification and check a reliable callsign database if you are unsure.

Looking up a callsign

Online callsign databases such as QRZ.com ↗ can provide information voluntarily published by operators. Regulatory databases may provide authoritative licensing information in some countries. A callsign lookup is useful, but never treat personal details from a database as an invitation to disclose them on air.
