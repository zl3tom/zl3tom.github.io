Network Radios Explained: Are They Amateur Radio?

Understand network radios, Zello push-to-talk, Wi-Fi and mobile-data radios, how they differ from RF amateur transceivers, and how communities such as IRN and ZMR use them.

← All guides

What is a network radio?

A network radio is usually a phone-like or dedicated push-to-talk device that sends voice over the internet rather than directly over an amateur RF frequency. Devices can use Wi-Fi or cellular data such as 4G and 5G. Many resemble handheld radios and have a physical PTT button.

Is a network radio amateur radio?

The device itself is not necessarily transmitting on an amateur RF band. A Zello-to-Zello conversation is internet voice/PTT. However, internet systems can be connected to amateur-radio gateways, repeaters, reflectors or nodes. Once a path crosses onto amateur RF, amateur-radio licensing and operating requirements apply to that RF side and network rules may also restrict access to licensed operators.

Zello and push-to-talk

Zello turns compatible phones and network-radio devices into internet PTT clients. Communities can organise channels for conversation and, where authorised, gateways can link services together.

Dedicated network-radio handsets

Some Android-based handsets look like traditional portable radios but contain cellular/Wi-Fi hardware rather than an amateur VHF/UHF transmitter. A hardware PTT key, loud speaker and rugged body can make them feel more radio-like than a normal phone.

IRN, ZMR and other communities

The International Radio Network (IRN) provides an internet-linked radio community with several connection methods. ZMR — Zello Mode Radio — uses Zello and also has links into digital-radio systems. The Original Network Radios Channel Suite is another Zello-based network-radio community. Availability, rooms, gateways and access requirements change, so always use each network's current website.

Network radio versus an RF handheld

An RF handheld can communicate directly over radio spectrum and may work locally without the public internet. A network radio normally needs an IP connection and server/service infrastructure. The two worlds can meet through gateways, which is why it is important to know where your audio is being routed.

Using QSO One with internet-linked radio

If you want to explore amateur-radio systems from a phone or other supported device, also see my QSO One Guide. It explains how to get started with QSO One and use its supported linked-radio features.

Good operating

Read the channel rules, identify appropriately, listen before transmitting, avoid long tests on busy channels, leave gaps between overs and remember that a linked channel may have listeners on several different systems.
